Section 1: History of Atomic Models
Problem 1: Entry Level (The First Idea) Who proposed that matter is made of tiny, indivisible particles called "atomos," and why is his idea considered philosophy rather than science?
Identify: Democritus. Explain: He proposed the concept of the indivisible atom around 400 BCE. It is considered philosophy because he never tested his ideas through experimentation or observation.
Result: Democritus; lacked experimental evidence.
Problem 2: Medium Level (The Discovery of the Nucleus) Ernest Rutherford's gold foil experiment changed our understanding of the atom. What was the unexpected observation, and what did it prove?
Identify: Most particles passed through, but some bounced straight back. Explain: If the atom were a "plum pudding" (diffuse positive charge), all particles should have passed through. The bounce-back proved that a small, dense, positively charged nucleus exists at the center.
Result: Bounced particles proved the existence of a dense nucleus.
Problem 3: Difficult Level (The Modern View) Contrast the Bohr model of the atom with the modern Quantum Model. What is the fundamental difference in how they describe electron movement?
Bohr Model: Electrons move in fixed, circular orbits (like planets). Quantum Model: Electrons exist in "orbitals"—regions of probability where an electron is likely to be found. Contrast: Bohr's model is deterministic (fixed paths); the Quantum model is probabilistic (clouds of probability).
Result: Fixed orbits vs. probability clouds.
Section 2: Atomic Notation and Mass
Problem 4: Entry Level (Subatomic Basics) What are the three subatomic particles, their charges, and their locations in the atom?
Proton: Positive (+1), located in the nucleus. Neutron: Neutral (0), located in the nucleus. Electron: Negative (-1), located in orbitals around the nucleus.
Result: Protons(+)/Neutrons(0) in nucleus; Electrons(-) in orbitals.
Problem 5: Medium Level (Isotope Calculation) An isotope of Carbon has a mass number of 14. Given that Carbon always has an atomic number of 6, how many protons, neutrons, and electrons does this neutral atom have?
Protons: Atomic number = 6. Electrons: Neutral atom = Protons = 6. Neutrons: Mass Number (14) - Atomic Number (6) = 8.
Result: 6 protons, 8 neutrons, 6 electrons.
Problem 6: Difficult Level (Clinical Application - Radioisotopes) In nuclear medicine, a specific radioisotope is used for imaging. Why does the number of neutrons specifically make an isotope "unstable" or radioactive, and how does this relate to the mass number?
Analysis: Isotopes have the same protons (Z) but different neutrons (N). Explain: A specific ratio of neutrons to protons is required for nucleus stability. When the neutron count is too high or too low for the number of protons present, the nucleus becomes unstable (radioactive). This change in neutron count directly changes the Mass Number (A).
Result: Unstable neutron-to-proton ratios lead to radioactivity.
Section 3: Energy Levels & Orbitals
Problem 7: Entry Level (Sublevel Capacity) How many electrons can fit into any single "s" sublevel? How many can fit into a "p" sublevel?
s-sublevel: Max 2 electrons. p-sublevel: Max 6 electrons.
Result: s = 2, p = 6.
Problem 8: Medium Level (The Nested Structure) Describe the order in which electrons fill the atom's space. What is the name of the principle that dictates this "inside-out" filling?
Identify: Lowest energy levels are filled first (1s, then 2s, then 2p...). Principle: The Aufbau Principle. Explain: Electrons naturally occupy the state of lowest energy before moving to higher, more distant shells.
Result: Filled from lowest energy to highest (Aufbau Principle).
Problem 9: Difficult Level (Orbital Rules) Explain the difference between the Pauli Exclusion Principle and Hund's Rule when filling a "p" sublevel.
Pauli Exclusion: Each individual orbital can hold only 2 electrons, and they must have opposite spins. Hund's Rule: Electrons fill empty orbitals in the same sublevel singly first (with parallel spins) before pairing up.
Result: Pauli governs the limit per orbital; Hund's governs the order of filling within a sublevel.
Section 4: Electron Configurations
Problem 10: Entry Level (Basic Configuration) Write the full electron configuration for Beryllium (Be), which has an atomic number of 4.
Electron Count: 4. Filling Order: 1s (2), 2s (2).
Result: 1s2 2s2
Problem 11: Medium Level (Applying the Rules) Write the full electron configuration for Phosphorus (P), which has an atomic number of 15.
Electron Count: 15. Filling Order: 1s2 → 2s2 → 2p6 → 3s2 → 3p3. Sum Check: 2+2+6+2+3 = 15.
Result: 1s2 2s2 2p6 3s2 3p3
Problem 12: Difficult Level (Configuring Ions) Oxygen (O) has an atomic number of 8. Write the configuration for a neutral Oxygen atom, and then write the configuration for an Oxide ion (O2-).
Neutral Oxygen: 8 electrons → 1s2 2s2 2p4. Oxide Ion (O2-): The 2- charge means it has gained 2 electrons. Total electrons = 10. Ion Configuration: 1s2 2s2 2p6.
Result: Neutral: 1s2 2s2 2p4; Ion: 1s2 2s2 2p6
